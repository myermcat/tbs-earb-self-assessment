Domains and categories

Two ways of grouping the same 176 questions. One builds the score. The other answers the questions the score cannot.

A domain is where a question lives. A category is what a question is about.

Every question has exactly one domain and any number of categories. That is the whole distinction, and everything below follows from it.

Four boxes, 25% each. A question cannot be in two at once, which is what makes the four add up to one score.

And it carries as many labels as apply

Three of the nine apply to this one. Its own domain is always one of them and nobody types it.

If it helps to say it out loud: a domain is the box you throw the ball into, and a ball cannot be in two boxes. A category is an attribute of the ball. It can be small and green and anything else that is true of it.

In the code this is the difference between the object a question belongs to and the properties it has.

Why both exist

Because the score cannot answer “how are we doing on security?” The 25 security questions are spread across all four domains, so no single domain is more than a quarter security. A department that is weak on it sees four domain bars that each look acceptable, because the weakness has been divided by four.

The Security category puts all 25 back together into one number, weighted exactly as they are weighted in their own domains. Same for Privacy, and for anything else worth asking about across the whole instrument.

Cyber is a category, not a domain, for the same reason. Made a domain, it would take questions away from Data, Application and Technology, where they also belong. As a category it takes nothing from anyone.

The arithmetic

A question counts once in the overall score, through its domain. It counts at full weight inside every category it carries.

176
questions, each in exactly one domain
227
category memberships across the same 176
130 carry one · 42 carry two · 4 carry three or more

The category scores therefore do not add up to the overall, and the tool says so in a line under them. They are a second cut through the same answers, never a second spine.

How the assignments get in

One column on each of the four domain sheets in the workbook. A question stays on its own tab; the ticks say what else it is about. The Topics column is a formula and fills itself.

Data & Information Architecture
Q#Assessment Question SecurityPrivacyFinancial Topics
Q27 Are data retention and disposition policies enforced… xx Security, Privacy
Q28 Are data quality rules defined and measured…

An empty row is the normal case and means “this one is only about its own domain”. That is 130 of the 176. The ticks are dropdowns, so a category cannot be mistyped, and a name the tool does not recognise refuses the whole import and says which question it was in.

Never duplicate a question onto a second tab

A question that appears on two sheets is counted twice in the overall score, and a department’s number moves because of how we filed a question. One question, one tab, as many category ticks as apply.

Still open. The five categories that are not domains are currently derived from the wording of each question, by us, and carry a “provisional grouping” badge wherever they appear in the tool. Dan’s own assignments come from the Categories column in the workbook.

Two of them rest on very little. Accessibility is asked about by three questions and Official Languages by two. A score built on two questions moves a long way on one answer, so the tool says so on the page rather than letting the number stand on its own. An earlier version of this page said both were empty; that was wrong, and it was wrong because the sweep counted hits without reading them. “Accessibility” the noun and WCAG are always the policy sense; “accessible” the adjective is the one that also means findable data, in the FAIR principles and in data reaching other departments.
